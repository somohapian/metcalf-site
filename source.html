THE METHOD · LETTER ONE

How do you find qualified candidates for a small business?

Finding candidates takes steady work, and it is easy to put off when you are running a business. Start here if your search is not bringing you people worth interviewing.

Start with the work the person needs to do. Make the job clear in your posting and careers page, ask for referrals, and contact people with relevant experience. Compare which routes bring you qualified conversations before spending more on advertising.

Why are applications coming in, but few candidates qualify?

When applications are coming in but few look suitable, it is tempting to assume the job board is the problem. First, look at what a candidate sees: the job ad, your careers page, and online reviews. Do they explain the work and give someone a believable reason to join you?

Next, check whether your requirements, compensation, location, and schedule fit the people you want to reach. Qualified people can be actively looking or happily employed. An application tells you someone is interested; it does not tell you whether they can do the work. Direct outreach helps you reach people who may never see the ad.

Should you post a job or contact candidates directly?

Posting a role lets interested candidates come to you. Direct outreach means identifying people whose experience fits and contacting them, including people who are not looking at job ads. Use both where they help, and compare the quality of conversations each produces.

Use these steps to decide whom to contact and where to spend your time:

1. Define the work and required skills

Before writing the ad, describe the work and the skills someone needs to do it. Look at relevant backgrounds to understand where those skills are found, while staying open to people who arrived there through a different route.

2. Find people with relevant experience

Look through professional networks, trade associations, industry groups, and contacts among suppliers and customers. Trade schools may be useful for entry-level openings. Match the channel to the experience the job requires.

3. Reach out personally

Explain why you are contacting this person, what the role involves, and why it might interest them. Make it easy to ask a question or decline.

4. Ask employees for referrals

Tell your team what the role needs and ask whether they know someone who could do it well. Explain any referral incentive and use the same assessment criteria for every applicant.

5. Make the careers page useful

Give people a clear picture of the company, the work, and current opportunities. Make applying straightforward and explain what happens next.

6. Review what each channel produces

Keep useful postings running while you test other ways to reach candidates. Choose the mix based on the role and your results, rather than assuming one channel will fill every opening.

How should you budget for finding candidates?

List the costs you can control: advertising, tools, recruiting support, and your team’s time reviewing applicants and interviewing. Start with a small number of channels that fit the role. Record what you spend and how many candidates meet the requirements.

If applications are plentiful but few people qualify, review the job details and targeting before increasing the budget. If qualified people are viewing the posting but not applying, check the pay, schedule, location, and application process. When comparing outside help, ask what work is included and which costs are additional.

Example: a technician opening attracts the wrong applicants

Illustrative example: an HVAC business is getting applications for a service technician, but few applicants have the experience the job needs. The owner reviews the title and requirements, explains the work more clearly, and checks that the careers page answers questions about schedule, equipment, and the team.

Alongside the revised posting, the business asks for referrals and contacts technicians whose experience looks relevant. It compares qualified conversations from each route. This is an example of how to diagnose a search, not a reported client result.

Common questions about finding qualified candidates

Why isn't anyone applying to my job posting?

Start by checking whether people are seeing the post, opening it, and completing an application. Low visibility calls for a look at the title and posting channels. Views without applications call for a look at the opportunity: pay, location, schedule, requirements, and how easy it is to apply. Then read your online reviews and careers page. Do they give a candidate a believable reason to choose your company?

How do I write a job posting that attracts qualified candidates?

Use a familiar, accurate job title, then explain why someone would want this job at your company. Describe the work, the team, and what the person can accomplish. Separate the requirements someone needs on day one from skills you can teach. Be clear about location, schedule, and compensation. Read the ad alongside your careers page and employee reviews: the story should hold together. A job ad should help someone picture working with you, rather than reproduce an internal job description.

How can a small business compete with big companies for talent?

Be specific about what your company offers. That might be direct access to the owner, a wider range of work, a predictable schedule, or a chance to see the results of a decision. Use examples you can deliver on. A small company will not be the right choice for everyone, and you still need a workable compensation package. Find out which parts of the opportunity matter to each candidate.

Where can I find employees for my small business?

Look at where people doing this kind of work spend time: professional associations, trade schools, local groups, industry job boards, LinkedIn, and your own careers page. Ask recent hires where they found opportunities. Test a small number of relevant channels and track qualified conversations and hires, not just application counts. Employee referrals and direct outreach can supplement the people who find your posting.

Why is my job posting attracting unqualified applicants?

Check three things first: your online reviews, the job description, and the careers page. Does each give a good candidate a reason to work here instead of somewhere else? Then look at the applicants against the actual requirements. An unclear title, unrealistic requirements, or a mismatch in pay, location, or schedule can all contribute. Improve the story and the specifics before simply buying more traffic. If the candidates you need still are not finding you, add direct outreach and referrals.

How can I find candidates beyond job boards?

Combine three routes: help candidates find you through job ads and your careers page, reach out directly to people whose experience fits, and ask your team for referrals. Start with a clear description of the work and the skills it requires. Keep track of where qualified conversations come from, then adjust your effort. The right mix depends on the role and the market.

Are employee referrals enough to fill open roles?

Referrals may fill an individual role, but relying on them alone limits you to the people your team already knows. Give employees a clear description of the job and any referral incentive. Assess referred candidates against the same job requirements as everyone else, and keep other sourcing channels open so you have a useful choice.

How much should a small business spend on recruiting?

Build the budget around the role and the work needed to fill it. Include advertising, recruiting support, tools, and the time your team spends reviewing and interviewing. Compare qualified candidates and hiring results by channel before increasing spend. A steady effort to maintain your careers page and candidate relationships can help between searches, but there is no single monthly budget that fits every small business.

What is a talent pool and how do I build one?

A talent pool is a list of people who may fit future openings and are open to hearing from you. Start with past finalists, referrals, and people you meet through outreach. Record the work they want, relevant experience, when you last spoke, and any communication preferences. Keep the information current and reach out when you have something relevant. A list you never revisit will not help much when a role opens.
