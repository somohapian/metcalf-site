HIRING GUIDE · OFFERS

Make a job offer the candidate can decide on.

Discuss the work, pay and expectations before sending the letter. Give the candidate a clear picture of the job, answer their concerns and agree on the next step.

A useful offer conversation covers why the person is considering a move, what matters to them and whether your job meets those needs. Confirm the pay, responsibilities and start date before putting the terms in writing. Stay in touch through the decision and the first day.

Find out what would make the move worthwhile

Ask why the candidate is considering leaving and what they want from their next job. Discuss compensation and the working arrangements early enough to identify a mismatch. Before preparing the offer, check what questions remain and whether the candidate is still interested.

When a candidate hesitates or changes their mind

A delayed answer or a withdrawal can have different causes. Ask what changed before deciding how to respond.

  • The counteroffer. The candidate’s employer offers changes that make staying more appealing. Ask what changed and how it affects the reasons they were considering a move.
  • No reply or a delayed decision. A candidate stops replying or delays the decision. Check in with a clear next step and invite an honest answer. Silence alone does not tell you why they are hesitating.
  • A withdrawal after acceptance. A candidate withdraws after accepting. Personal circumstances, competing offers, or unresolved concerns can all contribute. Find out what you can learn without assuming every withdrawal was preventable.

What to discuss before sending the offer

Work through these points with the candidate so the written offer reflects what you have discussed.

1. Explain the pay and the rest of the package

Discuss the full package: base pay, variable pay such as bonuses or commission, benefits, time off, schedule, and the work itself. Be clear about what is guaranteed and what depends on performance or other conditions. Ask which details matter to the candidate instead of assuming a benefit makes up for a salary difference.

2. Confirm the details before writing the letter

Talk through compensation, responsibilities, reporting line, start date, and relevant terms before sending the offer. Give the candidate time to ask questions. The letter should put that understanding in writing.

See an example offer letter to review how the details can be organized. If you want to prepare an offer in MS Rosie, our hiring software, offer letters are included in MS Rosie Starter’s free plan.

3. Address the counteroffer before it arrives

Ask early why the person is considering leaving and what would need to change for them to stay. Help them compare your role and their current job plainly. Do not assume their employer will make a particular counteroffer or that more money is the only factor.

4. Stay connected between acceptance and the start date

Agree on a start date that works for both sides, including notice and other commitments. Keep in touch, provide a named contact, and explain the first day. Use the time to prepare equipment, access, and a useful onboarding plan.

5. Understand the candidate’s other options

Ask what else the candidate is considering and when they need to decide. Having other offers does not establish someone’s quality, and having none does not count against them. Use the conversation to understand their decision and whether your role meets their priorities.

The counteroffer conversation

Ask early: “What would have to change for you to stay where you are?” Follow the answer with a discussion of what they want from the move. Work, pay, management, schedule, and growth may carry different weight for different people.

When a counteroffer arrives, return to that conversation. What changed? Which concerns remain? Explain what your job offers and what it does not. Make an offer you have good reason to believe they want, while leaving the candidate free to make their decision.

Example: when schedule matters as much as salary

Illustrative example: a controller candidate says a more predictable schedule matters as much as salary. Before offering, the owner explains month-end expectations and checks whether the actual schedule meets that need. If the current employer later offers a raise, both sides already understand the larger decision. This is an example of the conversation, not a client result.

Common questions about job offers and counteroffers

How do I make a competitive job offer a candidate wants to accept?

The offer should confirm a conversation you have already had, rather than reveal whether the candidate wants the job. Start early: learn why they are considering a move, what they want from the work, and what would make the change worthwhile. Explain plainly where your role meets those priorities and where it does not. Confirm the package, responsibilities, and start date before sending the letter. Aim to understand their decision before you make the offer, while recognizing that circumstances can still change.

How much room should I leave for salary negotiation when making an offer?

Agree on a realistic budget and discuss expectations before the final interview. Make a fair offer you can explain, taking the responsibilities, relevant experience, and your existing team into account. Do not leave an arbitrary percentage for negotiation or start low simply to see what the candidate will accept. If their expectations are outside the budget, address that early. Be clear about which parts of the package are flexible.

Why do candidates reject job offers or change their minds after accepting?

The reasons vary: compensation, a competing offer, a counteroffer, personal circumstances, or doubts about the work or manager. Ask what changed without turning the conversation into an argument. Record patterns across declined offers so you can improve the process. Early conversations about priorities and regular contact through the start date can surface concerns, but they cannot eliminate every change of mind.

How do I handle a counteroffer from the candidate's current employer?

Start that conversation before there is a counteroffer. Understand why the candidate is considering leaving and what they want to be different. Help them compare your opportunity with their current job plainly and honestly. If a counteroffer arrives, ask what it changes for them rather than assuming it is only about money. Revisit the priorities you discussed and the offer you can responsibly make. The candidate needs to choose the role that works for them.

Should I lead with the compensation number or explain the role first?

Discuss both early enough for the candidate to decide whether continuing makes sense. Learn what they want from the move, explain the work and the package, and answer questions directly. At the offer stage, connect the details to the priorities you have already discussed. Neither a persuasive pitch nor a salary number should conceal a mismatch.

How do I make a competitive offer when I can't beat a corporate salary?

Be honest about the pay and specific about the rest of the opportunity. Schedule, commute, responsibilities, manager access, flexibility, and benefits may matter, but only if they are real and valuable to this candidate. Ask what they would be giving up as well as what they would gain. If the overall package does not meet their needs, a clearer explanation alone may not solve it.

My offer was accepted. Am I done?

Keep in touch until the person starts. Confirm the start date, who will meet them, what to bring, and what the first day will look like. Make sure equipment, access, and the manager’s time are ready. Give the new hire a named contact for questions. Those practical steps help the move feel organized and connect the offer to a useful onboarding plan.

What should a small business offer letter include?

Set out the role, reporting line, compensation, proposed start date, and response deadline clearly. Include any conditions and employment terms that apply, with supporting benefits or incentive-plan information where needed. The letter should match what you discussed. Have the person responsible for your employment documents review it for your location and circumstances before sending it; a general template will not cover every situation.
