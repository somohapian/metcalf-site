The STACK Method

Find the right people. Keep the process moving. Make decisions from evidence. Help the hire work out.

STACK is our five-part hiring method. Hiring gets easier to manage when each part has a clear next step. Use this guide to prepare for a search, compare notes with your team, and follow through after someone starts.

Five parts of the work

  1. SourceFind people who can do the work.
  2. TempoGive every next step an owner and a date.
  3. AssessConnect experience to the job in front of you.
  4. CloseUnderstand the move before making the offer.
  5. KeepStay connected, make work better, follow through.

Print a working sheet

Interview worksheet · Candidate follow-up tracker

Use it yourself or with your team

These are working prompts, not a formula that guarantees a hire. Choose questions and checks that fit the actual job. Keep factual notes and distinguish what you know from what you still need to check.

Metcalf SearchThe STACK Method

01 / Source

Find people who can do the work.

Start with the work you need done. Then choose where to look.

Define the opening

Write down the important outcomes, the skills needed on day one, and what someone can learn. Be clear about pay, schedule, location and the daily work. Separate a real requirement from a preference.

Use more than one route

  • Post a clear description where people with relevant experience are likely to look.
  • Contact people whose work suggests a possible fit. Explain why you reached out and give them an easy way to decline.
  • Ask employees and trusted contacts for referrals. Describe the work, not just the title.
  • Give interested people a useful careers page with the opening, practical details and a clear way to apply.

Review what each channel produces

Count relevant conversations and candidates who meet the agreed criteria. A large application count alone does not tell you whether the channel is working. Adjust the message or channel when the evidence calls for it.

Before you spend more

If applicants keep missing the same requirement, check whether the posting makes that requirement clear. If suitable people decline, ask what made the role unattractive. The problem may be the offer, not the advertising.

Decide before launch

What must this person accomplish? What will you teach? Which channels will you try first? Who will review the results, and when?

02 / Tempo

Keep the next step clear.

Candidates should know what happens next. Your team should know who owns it.

Set the process before interviews begin

Agree on the interview stages, decision-makers, checks and offer approvals. Reserve time for feedback. Keep the process proportionate to the role.

Keep one useful record

  • Candidate and role.
  • Current stage and the latest conversation.
  • Next action, owner and promised date.
  • Questions still open and the information needed to answer them.

Use a dependable rhythm

Acknowledge suitable applicants promptly. Collect interview feedback within twenty-four hours when possible. Tell the candidate when they should hear from you, even if the next update is that a decision needs more time.

Send a written offer after the necessary checks and approvals are complete. Speed should remove waiting and repeated work, not skip a useful check.

When a step stalls

Name the missing decision or information. Give it an owner and a realistic date. Update the candidate before the promised response date passes.

A useful weekly review

Which candidates are waiting? What did we promise? Which role has no next action? Which approval could delay an offer?

03 / Assess

Ask for evidence. Then test the transfer.

A familiar job title is a starting point. Find out what the person can do in your job.

Use the Behavioral Bridge

Connect a real past example to the work in your role.

  1. Start with a specific past example. What happened, what did the candidate do, and what was the result?
  2. Identify the skill. How did they set priorities, make a decision or solve the problem?
  3. Apply it to your job. Describe a concrete task they would face and ask how they would use that approach.
  4. Ask what must change. What is different here? What would they need to learn or get help with?

Illustrative example

A dispatcher candidate has managed retail schedules. Ask about a day when someone called out. Then ask how they would handle a missing technician, a full schedule and an urgent service call. Explore travel time, required skills and what information they would need.

Keep separate questions separate

Can they do the work? Do they want it? Will the working environment suit them? How do they take responsibility when things go wrong? For accountability, ask what they could influence, what they did and what happened.

Record evidence against agreed criteria

Use consistent core questions and role-related rating criteria. Add a short relevant work sample or references where useful. Record gaps and follow-up questions before discussing the candidate with others. A total score, personality label, or whether a candidate says I or we should not decide the hire.

04 / Close

Understand the move before the offer.

A good offer answers the candidate's real questions about the work and the change.

Ask what would make the move worthwhile

Discuss pay, schedule, responsibilities, manager support, location and the opportunity to learn. Ask which details matter most. Do not assume a higher salary resolves every concern.

Confirm the details

  • Role, reporting relationship and work location.
  • Pay, incentives and any conditions that affect them.
  • Schedule, travel and other practical expectations.
  • Benefits details and when eligibility begins.
  • Start date, required checks and who will answer questions.

Talk through hesitation

Ask what is still unresolved and what other options the candidate is considering. Discuss how they would think about a counteroffer before one arrives. Give them room to make a considered decision.

Illustrative example

A candidate wants a more dependable schedule. Confirm the actual hours and how last-minute changes are handled before sending an offer. A vague promise now can become a problem after they start.

Stay connected after acceptance

Confirm the first-day plan and a contact for questions. Prepare the manager, tools, access and introductions. Check in during the notice period without pressuring the person to ignore their current obligations.

05 / Keep

Help the work and the relationship last.

Keep asks two questions regularly: is the work going well, and is this still a job the person wants to keep?

Stay connected

Keep a dependable time to talk. Recognize a specific contribution, ask what is working, and make room for what the employee wants to discuss. Review anything left open from last time.

Support new hires through the first ninety days, then continue regular conversations. Give annual milestones room for progress and growth. Do not save every concern for those dates.

Make work better

Turn what you hear into a practical change: clearer priorities, the right tool, time to practice or a new responsibility. Explain when a request cannot be met. The manager owns expectations and support. The employee owns the agreed work, questions and next steps.

Follow through

Record the promise, its owner and a review date. Return to it next time. If something is delayed, agree on what happens next.

Check the effect, not just the action

Finishing training does not establish that the work is going better. Ask what changed, what each person has observed and what remains unresolved before closing an issue.

When a concern needs a closer look

Describe the expected work and specific observations. Separate firsthand facts from reports and assumptions. Hear both perspectives and check expectations, resources, training and support before deciding what needs to change.

Use the regular check-in sheet or use the concern and follow-through sheet. Both work without hiring software.

Assess / Working sheet 2 of 2

Compare evidence with the criteria.

Candidate / role
Interviewer / date

Agree on role-related criteria before interviewing. Use the same core questions and criteria for each candidate. Define any rating scale before using it.

Check four separate areas

  • Can: Can they do the essential work? What evidence shows it?
  • Will: Do they want the actual work, schedule and responsibilities?
  • Fit: Do the working conditions and support match what they need?
  • Accountability: What could they influence, what did they do, and what did they learn?

Fit means the working environment, not similarity to the interviewer. A personality label, total score, or whether a candidate says I or we should not decide the hire.
