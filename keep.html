THE METHOD · LETTER FIVE

Keep your people productive, satisfied, and engaged.

Employee retention for a small business starts with the work and the people doing it. Stay connected, make work better, and follow through, from the first weeks on the job to the people who have been with you for years.

Keep is the fifth letter of the STACK Method. It is the ongoing work of helping people do well and have reasons to stay. Ask two questions: Is the work going well? And is this still a job the person wants to keep? Both matter, for new hires and existing employees.

Employee retention is an ongoing part of the job

Keep is the fifth letter of the Free hiring guides. A good hire needs clear work, useful support, and a reason to keep choosing the job. Someone who has been with you for years needs those things too.

Start with ordinary conversations while things are going well. Recognize useful work, ask what the person wants to learn, and notice what makes the job satisfying. A check-in can be brief when there is nothing that needs a deeper conversation.

Two questions to keep asking

Is the work going well? Talk about the results expected, what has happened, and what is helping or getting in the way. Use specific examples so both people understand the work being discussed.

Is this still a job the person wants to keep? Ask what they enjoy, what they want more opportunity to do, and what would make the job better. Workload, schedule, pay, recognition, growth, and the manager relationship may carry different weight for different people.

Keep both questions open. Someone can be doing excellent work and still want to leave. Someone can want to stay and need help doing the work well.

Three moves that make Keep practical

1. Stay connected

Keep a dependable time to talk. Ask what is going well, recognize a specific contribution, and check on anything left open from the last conversation. Make room for what the employee wants to discuss as well as what the manager needs to cover.

Use the first ninety days to help a new hire settle in, then continue with regular check-ins and an annual conversation about the work, progress, and what comes next. A change in responsibilities or a concern from either person is a reason to talk sooner.

2. Make work better

Turn what you hear into something useful: a clearer priority, access to the right tool, time to practice, recognition for good work, or a chance to take on a new responsibility. Agree on a change that fits the work and explain when a request cannot be met.

Both people have responsibilities. The manager sets clear expectations, provides support, and follows through on commitments. The employee does the agreed work, raises questions or barriers, and follows through on their own next steps.

3. Follow through

Keep a short record of open issues, promises, who owns each action, and the date you will review it. Start the next check-in by returning to that record. If something is delayed, say so and agree on what happens next.

Completing an action does not necessarily resolve the issue. Training may be finished while the person still needs practice. A tool may have been ordered while the work is still blocked. Ask what changed in the work and how each person sees the situation before calling it resolved.

When something needs a closer look

A routine check-in may uncover a concern about the work or the experience of doing it. Make time for a deeper conversation when it is needed. Describe the expected work and the specific result or behavior that prompted the concern.

Separate what you saw firsthand from what someone reported to you. Ask the employee for their account, hear the manager’s perspective, and identify what still needs to be checked. Avoid turning an interpretation into an established fact.

Before reaching a conclusion, check whether expectations were understood, priorities were realistic, and the person had the resources, training, and manager support needed. Then agree on what each person will do and when you will review progress. Keep the concern open until you have checked whether the change helped.

A practical example

Illustrative example, not a client result. An experienced service coordinator has missed two promised customer updates. The manager brings the agreed update times and the messages they reviewed, then asks the coordinator what happened. A technician has also reported a delay, which the manager identifies as a report to check rather than something they personally observed.

The conversation reveals that job-status information arrives late and that the coordinator is unsure which calls take priority. The manager agrees to clarify priorities and the handoff. The coordinator agrees to flag missing information earlier. They name owners and a review date, then check whether customers actually receive updates on time. Writing the new handoff instructions alone would not close the issue.

The same conversation can recognize the coordinator’s strong customer relationships and explore their interest in training new staff. Keep gives attention to what is working and what could make the job more worthwhile, alongside any concern that needs fixing.

Start well, then keep going

For new hires, prepare equipment, introductions, training, and a clear first assignment before they arrive. Set realistic thirty-, sixty-, and ninety-day expectations, with time to compare progress and adjust support. The assessment and offer conversations should give the person an accurate picture of the work they are joining.

For established employees, revisit expectations when the work changes. Keep recognizing contributions, discussing growth, and reviewing promises between annual milestones. A long tenure is not a reason to assume you already know what matters to someone.

Common questions about Keep

Why do good employees quit and how do I keep them?

People leave for different reasons, including the work, pay, schedule, manager relationship, growth opportunities, or circumstances outside the business. Keep asks two questions regularly: is the work going well, and is this still a job the person wants to keep?

Stay connected while things are healthy, recognize good work, and ask what would make the job more worthwhile. Agree on practical improvements and return to your promises. If there is a concern, hear both perspectives and check the facts before deciding what needs to change.

What is a stay interview and should I be doing them?

A stay interview asks what makes someone want to keep their job and what might change that. It is useful for experienced employees as well as new hires. Make it part of your regular conversations and take more time around ninety-day and annual milestones.

Ask: “What is working well for you?” “Which work would you like more opportunity to do?” “What makes the job harder than it needs to be?” “What would you like to learn?” and “What would make you consider leaving?” Recognize what the person contributes, agree on any next steps, and give each promise an owner and review date.

Why do new hires quit in the first 90 days?

The job may differ from what they expected, or they may need clearer priorities, better training, resources, or manager support. Pay, schedule, relationships, and personal circumstances can matter too. Ask the new hire and the manager what each expected and what each has experienced, using specific examples.

Check what was promised during hiring and what was actually provided. Agree on the support or changes needed and review whether they helped. Gallup’s onboarding guidance also emphasizes manager involvement, clear expectations, and working relationships.

What does a good onboarding process include for a small business?

Prepare the equipment, access, introductions, training, and first assignment before day one. Explain what good work looks like and who can help. Set realistic goals for thirty, sixty, and ninety days, with regular conversations between those milestones.

Ask both how the work is going and how the person feels about the job. The manager owns clear expectations and support. The new hire owns the agreed work, questions, and next steps. Record commitments and check their effect, then continue that routine beyond onboarding. Gallup describes onboarding as an ongoing process, with the manager and team involved.

How often should I do one-on-ones with a new hire?

A weekly check-in is a useful starting point during the first ninety days, with shorter conversations when someone needs help sooner. Keep a regular rhythm after that, adjusting it to the person and the work rather than stopping once they are established.

A healthy check-in can be short: what is going well, what deserves recognition, what would help, and what remains open from last time? Use a longer conversation when either person raises a concern or wants to discuss growth. Give ninety-day and annual milestones space without saving every issue for those dates.

How do I spot an employee about to quit?

A change in participation or missed commitments is a reason to ask how things are going, not proof that someone is about to leave. Describe what you have observed, distinguish it from reports or assumptions, and give the person room to explain.

Ask what they want from the job and what, if anything, is getting in the way. Hear the manager’s perspective too. Agree on any actions and review whether the work or experience improves. Regular conversations are useful even when there are no warning signs.

Put Keep to work in your business

You can start with a regular conversation and a short record of what each person agreed to do. Keep the two questions visible: is the work going well, and is this still a job the person wants to keep? Use the three moves to stay connected, make work better, and follow through.
